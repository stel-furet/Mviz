Introduction

Freque uses advanced audio analysis to create reactive visualizations that respond to music in real-time. This guide explains how the system detects and analyzes audio, and what information is available to your visualizations.

Think of Freque's audio system like a sophisticated music analyzer that listens to your audio and breaks it down into understandable pieces:

  • Frequencies - The different pitches and tones in the music
  • Energy - How loud or intense the music is
  • Beats - The rhythmic pulse of the music
  • Musical Notes - The specific notes and chords being played
  • Frequency Bands - Seven musical frequency ranges (sub-bass, bass, low-mid, mid, high-mid, treble, air)

All of this information is calculated once per frame and shared with all visualizations for optimal performance.

How Audio is Detected

Audio Input Setup

Freque uses the Web Audio API, which is built into modern web browsers, to capture and analyze audio. Here's how it works:

The Audio Context

When Freque starts, it creates an Audio Context - think of this as the "listening device" that connects to your audio source (whether it's a music file, microphone, or streaming audio).

Key Settings:

  • FFT Size: 8192 - This determines how detailed the frequency analysis is. Higher numbers = more detail but more processing power needed. 8192 gives us 4096 individual frequency "bins" to analyze.
  • Sample Rate: 44.1 kHz - This is the standard CD quality sample rate. It means the system analyzes 44,100 audio samples per second.
  • Frequency Range: 20 Hz to 22,050 Hz - This covers the full range of human hearing (from deep bass to high treble).

Stereo Support

Freque can analyze audio in two ways:

  • Mono Mode - Combines left and right channels into a single analysis
  • Stereo Mode - Analyzes left and right channels separately, allowing for visualizations that respond differently to each side

How Audio Connects

When you play music in Freque, the audio flows through this path:

  1. Audio Source (your music file, microphone, or other input device)
  2. Audio Context (the listening device)
  3. Channel Splitter (if stereo - separates left and right)
  4. Analyzers (the analysis engines that break down the audio)
  5. Frequency Data Arrays (the raw data that visualizations use)

This happens automatically - you don't need to do anything special. Just play music and Freque starts analyzing!

Frequency Data

How Frequency Data is Calculated

Freque breaks down the audio into 4096 individual frequency "bins" - each bin represents a small range of frequencies. This gives you incredibly detailed information about what's happening in the music at any moment.

Every frame (60 times per second), Freque:

  1. Takes a "snapshot" of the current audio
  2. Uses a mathematical process called FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) to break it into frequency components
  3. Converts this into an array of numbers from 0-255, where:
    • 0 = no sound at that frequency
    • 255 = maximum sound at that frequency

What You Get

Visualizations receive a frequencies array - this is a list of 4096 numbers, each representing the intensity of a specific frequency range at that moment. You can use this to:

  • Make different parts of your visualization respond to different frequencies
  • Create effects that "follow" the melody or bass line
  • Build visualizations that highlight specific instruments

Dominant Frequency (Spectral Centroid)

Freque calculates the dominant frequency using a technique called spectral centroid - a weighted average of all frequencies in the audio spectrum. Unlike simply finding the loudest frequency, spectral centroid provides a stable, accurate representation of the "center of mass" of the sound, weighted by energy (amplitude squared).

How it works: Instead of just picking the single loudest frequency, Freque calculates a weighted average where each frequency is weighted by its energy. This means:

  • Frequencies with more energy contribute more to the result
  • The result represents the overall "center" of the sound spectrum
  • It's more stable and less jittery than just finding the maximum value
  • It better represents the perceived pitch or tonal center of the music

Temporal Smoothing: To further reduce jitter and provide smooth, stable values, Freque applies exponential moving average smoothing to the spectral centroid. This means the dominant frequency value changes gradually over time rather than jumping around frame-by-frame.

How smoothing works: Each frame, the new spectral centroid value is blended with the previous smoothed value using an exponential moving average. The smoothing factor (88%) means that 88% of the previous value is retained and 12% of the new value is incorporated. This creates a smooth, gradual transition that:

  • Reduces rapid fluctuations and jitter from frame-to-frame
  • Maintains responsiveness to real frequency changes in the music
  • Provides stable values that better represent the overall tonal character
  • Results in smoother visual transitions when using dominant frequency for color or effects
  • Improves performance for animations that respond to frequency changes

When audio stops or no frequency is detected, the smoothed value decays gradually (rather than dropping instantly to zero), providing a more natural fade-out effect.

Why this is better than finding the loudest frequency:

  • More stable - doesn't jump around as much when multiple frequencies are present
  • More accurate - represents the overall tonal character, not just one peak
  • Better for color mapping - provides smoother transitions between colors
  • More musically meaningful - represents the "center" of the sound, not just a peak

It's useful for:

  • Color changes based on pitch (low = red, high = blue, etc.)
  • Effects that follow the main melody or tonal center
  • Visualizations that respond to the "center" of the sound spectrum
  • Smooth, stable frequency-based animations

Frequency Bands: Seven Musical Ranges

Freque automatically divides the frequency spectrum into seven bands using actual musical frequency ranges (Hz), not percentage-based divisions. This ensures consistent and musically accurate frequency band detection regardless of FFT size or sample rate:

  • Sub-Bass (20-60 Hz) - The deepest, lowest frequencies (subwoofer range, very deep bass). This range captures the fundamental frequencies of sub-bass instruments and the lowest harmonics.
  • Bass (60-250 Hz) - The deep, low frequencies (kick drums, bass guitars, low synths). This range captures the fundamental frequencies of bass instruments and percussion.
  • Low-Mid (250-500 Hz) - The lower middle frequencies (lower vocals, lower guitar strings, lower piano notes). This range captures the warmth and body of instruments.
  • Mid (500-2000 Hz) - The middle frequencies (vocals, guitars, most instruments). This range captures the "body" of the music where most melodic content lives.
  • High-Mid (2000-4000 Hz) - The upper middle frequencies (upper vocals, snare drums, presence range). This range captures clarity and presence in the mix.
  • Treble (4000-8000 Hz) - The high frequencies (cymbals, hi-hats, high synths, harmonics). This range captures brightness, sparkle, and high-frequency detail.
  • Air (8000-22050 Hz) - The highest frequencies (high harmonics, air, shimmer). This range captures the very top end and adds sparkle and airiness to the sound.

Why Hz-based instead of percentages? Using actual Hz ranges ensures that:

  • Each band always means the same frequency range, regardless of audio settings
  • Frequency bands are consistent across different FFT sizes
  • Visualizations respond to the correct musical frequency ranges
  • The system works accurately with different sample rates (44.1kHz, 48kHz, etc.)

Each band gives you a single number (0-1) representing the energy in that range. This makes it easy to create visualizations where:

  • Sub-Bass and Bass control size, position, or deep effects
  • Low-Mid and Mid control color, rotation, or body
  • High-Mid and Treble control brightness, detail, or sparkle
  • Air adds shimmer and high-frequency effects

Musical Notes Detection

What is Note Detection?

Freque can identify which musical chords and keys are being played in the audio. The harmonic analysis system uses frequency-domain analysis to detect:

  • Chords - Groups of notes played together (major, minor, diminished, augmented)
  • Musical Key - The overall key the music is in
  • Harmonic Progression - How chords change over time

How Note Detection Works

The system:

  1. Analyzes the frequency data to find which frequencies are strongest
  2. Converts those frequencies to musical notes (using the standard A4 = 440 Hz reference)
  3. Calculates note strengths for each of the 12 semitones (C, C#, D, D#, E, F, F#, G, G#, A, A#, B)
  4. Matches note patterns against chord templates to identify chords
  5. Analyzes chord progressions to determine the musical key

What You Can Do With Notes

Note detection enables advanced visualizations like:

  • Color by Chord - Different colors for different chord types (major = bright, minor = dark)
  • Chord Visualization - Visual patterns that match the chord structure
  • Key-Based Effects - Effects that change based on the musical key
  • Harmonic Reactivity - Visualizations that respond to chord changes

Availability: Harmonic data (chords, keys, progressions) is now available to all plugins through sharedAudioData.harmonic, independent of the AI Autopilot system. This means plugins can access chord and key information whether Autopilot is enabled or not.

Beat Detection

How Beat Detection Works

Freque uses a sophisticated frequency-weighted, bass-focused beat detection method that provides highly accurate beat detection across different music styles and genres.

Frequency-Weighted Bass-Focused Detection

Freque's enhanced beat detection focuses on bass frequencies (20-250 Hz) where beats are most prominent, rather than treating all frequencies equally. This approach provides several advantages:

  • More Accurate: Kick drums and snares are most prominent in bass frequencies, so focusing here reduces false positives from treble frequencies (cymbals, hi-hats)
  • Better for Bass-Heavy Music: Detects beats even when overall energy is low but bass is strong
  • Fewer False Positives: High treble sounds (cymbals, hi-hats) won't trigger false beats
  • Adaptive Intervals: Minimum time between beats adapts to the detected tempo, preventing false beats at different tempos

Multi-Factor Beat Detection

The system combines multiple factors to determine if a beat occurred:

  1. Bass Energy Increase (Primary, 60% weight) - How much bass energy increased compared to recent average
  2. Overall Energy Increase (Secondary, 20% weight) - How much overall energy increased
  3. Spectral Flux (Tertiary, 20% weight) - How much the frequency content changed (new sounds starting)

These factors are combined into a confidence score. If the confidence exceeds a dynamic threshold (which adapts to music characteristics), and enough time has passed since the last beat (based on detected tempo), a beat is detected.

Beat Strength and Confidence

The system provides two important metrics for each beat:

  • Beat Strength (0-1) - How strong the beat is, based on how much the confidence exceeded the threshold. Stronger beats have higher strength values.
  • Beat Confidence (0-1) - How confident the system is that a beat actually occurred, based on the combined factors. This value is calculated continuously, even when no beat is detected.

These metrics are useful for:

  • Making effects stronger on "stronger" beats
  • Filtering out false positives
  • Creating more nuanced visual responses
  • Understanding beat detection quality in real-time

Comparison with Industry Standards

Freque's beat detection approach is similar to methods used in professional audio software:

  • Similar to Ableton Live: Uses frequency-weighted detection and multi-factor analysis, though Ableton may use additional onset detection preprocessing
  • Industry Standard Approach: Focuses on bass frequencies where beats are most prominent, which is a common technique in professional audio analysis tools
  • Adaptive Thresholds: Dynamic threshold adjustment based on music characteristics (similar to professional DAWs)

What You Get From Beat Detection

Your visualizations receive:

  • beat - A true/false value indicating if a beat was detected this frame
  • beatStrength - A number (0-1) showing how strong the beat is
  • beatConfidence - A number (0-1) showing how confident the detection is
  • beatHistory - A list of recent beat timestamps (for tempo calculation)

Using Beats in Visualizations

Beats are perfect for:

  • Pulsing Effects - Make things grow or shrink on beats
  • Color Flashes - Bright color bursts on beats
  • Particle Bursts - Spawn particles or effects on beats
  • Transitions - Change visualization modes on beats
  • Rhythmic Patterns - Create patterns that match the music's rhythm

Energy Calculation

What is Energy in This Context?

Energy is a measure of how "intense" or "loud" the audio is at any moment. It's calculated by averaging all the frequency values together. Think of it as the "volume" of the music, but more nuanced - it considers all frequencies, not just overall loudness.

It's important to understand the difference between loudness and volume: Loudness is a subjective measure of how intensely our ears perceive a sound, while volume is a measure of the physical amplitude of a sound wave. Energy in Freque is more closely related to volume - it measures the actual physical intensity of the audio signal across all frequencies.

Overall Energy

The simplest energy calculation:

  1. Takes all frequency values (0-255 each)
  2. Sums them up
  3. Divides by the total number of frequencies
  4. Normalizes to 0-1 range

This gives you a single number representing the overall intensity of the music right now.

Frequency Band Energy

Freque calculates energy for each of the seven frequency bands separately using RMS (Root Mean Square) calculation:

Sub-Bass Energy (20-60 Hz)

Calculates the RMS energy in the sub-bass frequencies. This is useful for:

  • Effects that respond to very deep bass
  • Subwoofer-range vibrations
  • Deep, powerful visual effects

Bass Energy (60-250 Hz)

Calculates the RMS energy in the bass frequencies. This is useful for:

  • Effects that respond to kick drums
  • Low-frequency vibrations
  • Deep, powerful visual effects

Low-Mid Energy (250-500 Hz)

Calculates energy in the low-mid frequencies. This captures:

  • Lower vocals and instruments
  • Warmth and body
  • Lower guitar strings

Mid Energy (500-2000 Hz)

Calculates energy in the mid frequencies. This captures:

  • Vocals
  • Most instruments
  • The "body" of the music

High-Mid Energy (2000-4000 Hz)

Calculates energy in the high-mid frequencies. This captures:

  • Upper vocals
  • Snare drums
  • Presence and clarity

Treble Energy (4000-8000 Hz)

Calculates energy in the treble frequencies. This captures:

  • High-frequency details
  • Cymbals and hi-hats
  • Bright, sparkly sounds
  • Harmonics and overtones

Air Energy (8000-22050 Hz)

Calculates energy in the air frequencies. This captures:

  • Very high harmonics
  • Shimmer and airiness
  • Top-end sparkle

Note: All frequency band calculations use actual Hz ranges, ensuring musically accurate detection regardless of audio processing settings.

Dominant Frequency Range

Freque also determines which frequency band is currently dominant:

  • 'subBass' - Sub-bass frequencies are strongest
  • 'bass' - Bass frequencies are strongest
  • 'lowMid' - Low-mid frequencies are strongest
  • 'mid' - Mid frequencies are strongest
  • 'highMid' - High-mid frequencies are strongest
  • 'treble' - Treble frequencies are strongest
  • 'air' - Air frequencies are strongest

This is useful for automatic color scheme selection or mode switching based on the type of music playing.

Using Energy in Visualizations

Energy values are perfect for:

  • Size Control - Make things bigger when energy is high
  • Brightness Control - Make things brighter with more energy
  • Speed Control - Make animations faster with higher energy
  • Intensity Control - Control how strong effects are
  • Band-Specific Effects - Different effects for bass, mid, and treble

Additional Audio Features

Spectral Flux

Spectral Flux measures how much the frequency content of the music is changing. Think of it as detecting when new sounds start or when the music changes character.

How it works:

  1. Compares the current frequency spectrum to the previous frame
  2. Counts how many frequencies increased (new sounds starting)
  3. Normalizes to 0-1 range

Use cases:

  • Detecting when new instruments enter
  • Creating effects that respond to musical changes
  • Enhancing beat detection (beats often have high spectral flux)
  • Triggering transitions when music changes

Energy Change

Energy Change is the difference between the current energy and the previous frame's energy. It tells you if the music is getting louder or quieter.

Use cases:

  • Detecting crescendos (music getting louder)
  • Detecting drops (sudden energy changes)
  • Creating effects that respond to volume changes
  • Making visualizations more sensitive to dynamics

Tempo Detection

Freque uses advanced tempo detection methods similar to Ableton Live, combining histogram analysis with exponential decay weighting, continuous autocorrelation, and tempo persistence for accurate tempo estimation that works reliably even with complex rhythms, varying tempos, or sparse beat detection.

How Tempo Detection Works

Histogram-Based Tempo Detection with Exponential Decay (Primary Method)

When beats are being detected, the system uses an improved histogram method with exponential decay weighting (similar to Ableton Live's approach):

  1. Tracks Beat Intervals: Records the time between consecutive beats, but uses exponential decay weighting instead of a hard time cutoff
  2. Exponential Decay Weighting: Recent beats are weighted more heavily than older beats, with a 30-second half-life. This means beats from 30 seconds ago have 50% weight, beats from 60 seconds ago have 25% weight, etc.
  3. Builds a Weighted Histogram: Groups similar intervals together, but counts are weighted by beat age, finding the most common beat spacing
  4. Identifies Patterns: Finds the most frequent interval, which represents the underlying tempo
  5. Converts to BPM: Calculates BPM using the formula: BPM = 60,000 / interval_in_milliseconds
  6. Handles Multiples: Can detect half-time (slower) or double-time (faster) patterns, providing multiple tempo candidates
  7. Consistency Analysis: Factors in how stable the tempo has been over time for more reliable confidence scores

This method is highly accurate because it filters out irregular beats, weights recent information more heavily, and finds the consistent underlying pulse of the music.

Continuous Autocorrelation (Secondary Method)

Unlike a simple fallback, Freque runs autocorrelation continuously on energy data (similar to Ableton Live's Tempo Following feature):

  1. Continuous Energy Analysis: Continuously analyzes the overall energy level over time (up to 5 seconds of history)
  2. Periodic Autocorrelation: Calculates autocorrelation every second to identify repeating energy patterns
  3. Finds Repeating Patterns: Uses autocorrelation to identify repeating energy patterns that indicate tempo
  4. Estimates Tempo: Converts the pattern repetition rate to BPM
  5. Independent Operation: Works continuously, not just when beats are sparse, providing a second opinion on tempo

This method provides a reliable tempo estimate even for less percussive music (like ambient or orchestral pieces) where individual beats are harder to detect, and works continuously to validate beat-based detection.

Hybrid Method Combination

Freque combines both methods intelligently:

  • When Both Agree: If histogram and autocorrelation detect similar tempos (within 10 BPM), confidence is boosted by combining both results
  • Weighted Combination: Histogram results are weighted 60%, autocorrelation 40% when both are available
  • Fallback Behavior: If one method has low confidence, the other can still provide tempo information

Tempo Persistence

Freque maintains tempo persistence (similar to Ableton Live's behavior):

  • Persistent Tempo: The system maintains a "persistent tempo" that doesn't drop to zero when beats become sparse
  • Slow Decay: When confidence drops, the persistent tempo decays slowly (not instantly to zero)
  • Always Available: Even when current detection has low confidence, the persistent tempo is available for visualizations
  • Gradual Updates: Persistent tempo updates gradually when new high-confidence detections occur

This ensures that tempo information remains available even during quiet sections or when beat detection becomes less reliable.

Adaptive Tempo Smoothing

The detected tempo uses adaptive smoothing based on confidence and stability:

  • Stability Tracking: Tracks how stable the tempo has been over time (low variance = high stability)
  • Adaptive Smoothing: More smoothing for stable, high-confidence tempos (less jitter), more responsive for new or changing tempos
  • Smoothing Factor: Ranges from 0.85 (responsive) to 1.0 (very stable) based on confidence and stability
  • Stable BPM Values: Prevents sudden, jarring changes while still adapting to tempo variations

Tempo Confidence and Candidates

Freque provides comprehensive information about tempo detection:

  • Tempo Confidence (0-1): How confident the system is in the detected tempo. Calculated from peak strength, consistency, and method agreement. Higher values (closer to 1) mean more reliable detection.
  • Tempo Candidates: Multiple potential tempos with their confidence scores. This helps identify cases where the music might be at half-time or double-time.
  • Persistent Confidence: Even when current detection confidence is low, a decayed persistent confidence is available (never drops to zero instantly)

Comparison with Ableton Live

Freque's tempo detection shares many similarities with Ableton Live's Tempo Following and BeatSeeker features:

Similarities:

  • Exponential Decay Weighting: Both use exponential decay instead of hard time cutoffs (Ableton uses similar decay patterns)
  • Continuous Operation: Both operate continuously, not just when beats are detected
  • Tempo Persistence: Both maintain tempo even when detection confidence drops
  • Hybrid Methods: Both combine multiple detection methods for better accuracy
  • Adaptive Smoothing: Both use adaptive smoothing based on confidence and stability

Differences:

  • Onset Detection: Ableton may use additional onset/transient detection preprocessing (Freque focuses on beat intervals and energy patterns)
  • Real-Time Adaptation: Ableton's Tempo Following is designed for live performance scenarios with real-time tempo changes (Freque is optimized for visualization reactivity)
  • User Control: Ableton provides manual tempo adjustment and warping features (Freque focuses on automatic detection for visualization)

Overall, Freque's tempo detection provides professional-grade accuracy similar to Ableton Live, optimized for real-time music visualization applications.

Use cases:

  • Syncing animations to music speed
  • Adjusting effect timing based on tempo
  • Creating tempo-reactive visualizations
  • Intelligent mode switching based on music pace
  • Using confidence scores to decide when to trust the tempo value

Using Tempo in Visualizations

Here's an example of how to use tempo and tempo confidence in your visualizations:

// Get tempo and its confidence
const tempo = sharedAudioData.tempo;
const tempoConfidence = sharedAudioData.tempoConfidence;

if (tempoConfidence > 0.7) {
    // Use tempo for effects if confident
    console.log(`Current tempo: ${tempo} BPM with ${Math.round(tempoConfidence * 100)}% confidence`);
    
    // Example: Rotate speed based on tempo
    const rotationSpeed = tempo / 120; // Normalize to 120 BPM
    object.rotation += rotationSpeed * deltaTime;
} else {
    console.log('Tempo detection not confident, using fallback or previous tempo.');
}

// Check tempo candidates for alternative tempos
if (sharedAudioData.tempoCandidates && sharedAudioData.tempoCandidates.length > 0) {
    const topCandidate = sharedAudioData.tempoCandidates[0];
    console.log(`Top tempo candidate: ${topCandidate.bpm} BPM (${Math.round(topCandidate.confidence * 100)}% confidence)`);
}

Morph Intensity

Morph Intensity is a combined metric that measures how "interesting" the audio is right now. It combines:

  • Energy change (how much things are changing)
  • Spectral flux (how much frequencies are changing)
  • Silence boost (extra boost during quiet sections to keep things interesting)

Use cases:

  • Automatic color morphing (change colors when music gets interesting)
  • Effect intensity control
  • Mode switching triggers

Structure Detection

Through the AI Autopilot system, Freque can detect musical structure:

  • Intro - The beginning of the song
  • Verse - The main sections
  • Chorus - The repeated, usually more energetic sections
  • Bridge - The contrasting sections
  • Outro - The ending

This enables visualizations that respond to song structure, like using different effects for verses vs. choruses.

How to Access Audio Data

In your plugin's onUpdate or onRender method, you receive sharedAudioData as the third parameter:

onUpdate(deltaTime, timestamp, sharedAudioData) {
    // Get overall energy
    const energy = sharedAudioData.energy;
    
    // Get frequency bands
    const subBass = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.subBass;
    const bass = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.bass;
    const lowMid = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.lowMid;
    const mid = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.mid;
    const highMid = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.highMid;
    const treble = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.treble;
    const air = sharedAudioData.frequencyBands.air;
    
    // Check for beat
    if (sharedAudioData.beat) {
        // Do something on beat!
    }
    
    // Get raw frequency data
    const frequencies = sharedAudioData.frequencies;
    if (frequencies) {
        // Access individual frequency bins
        const bassBin = frequencies[0]; // First bin (lowest frequency)
        const trebleBin = frequencies[frequencies.length - 1]; // Last bin (highest frequency)
    }
}
